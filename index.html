Points, Lines & Planes

MA.912.GR.1 — The undefined terms that everything else is built from.

Point

A point has no size — no length, width, or depth. It only marks a location. We name it with a capital letter, like A.

Line

A line is a straight path of points that extends forever in both directions. It has length but no width. Any 2 points determine exactly one line — written AB or ℓ.

Plane

A plane is a flat surface that extends forever in every direction — like an infinite sheet of paper. It has length and width but no thickness. Any 3 non-collinear points determine exactly one plane.

Collinear points lie on the same line. Coplanar points/lines lie in the same plane. Drag the points in the 3D scene to test both ideas yourself.

Naming & Notation

Aa point
line AB or ℓa line through A and B (no endpoints, goes forever)
segment ABthe part of the line between A and B only
ray AB→starts at A, passes through B, goes forever one way
plane M / plane ABCa flat surface, named by a letter or 3 points on it
Postulates worth memorizing: (1) Through any 2 points there is exactly 1 line. (2) Through any 3 non-collinear points there is exactly 1 plane. (3) If 2 points lie in a plane, the entire line through them lies in that plane too. (4) Two distinct planes either don't meet, or meet in exactly 1 line.

Three points, floating freely in space — none of them are forced to line up.

Relationships Between Lines

  • Intersecting lines — cross at exactly one point.
  • Parallel lines — same plane, same direction, never intersect (symbol: ∥).
  • Skew lines — don't intersect and aren't parallel, because they live in different planes (only possible in 3D — this is why the 3D scene matters!).
  • Perpendicular lines — intersect to form a 90° angle (symbol: ⊥).

Why 3 Points Make a Plane

Think of a camera tripod: 3 legs always sit flat and stable no matter the ground, but a 4-legged stool can wobble. That's because 3 non-collinear points pin down exactly one flat surface — a 4th point might not lie on that same surface at all. Switch to "A Plane" above and rotate — notice the plane is completely determined by A, B, and C.

Segments, Distance & Midpoint

MA.912.GR.1 — Measuring the space between points.

Drag the cyan and magenta points anywhere on the grid.

Distance Formula

The distance between two points is the length of the segment connecting them — it comes straight from the Pythagorean theorem:

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Midpoint Formula

The midpoint is the point exactly halfway between two endpoints — just average the x's and average the y's:

M = ( (x₁+x₂)/2 , (y₁+y₂)/2 )
Point A(0, 0)
Point B(0, 0)
Distance AB0
Midpoint M(0, 0)
Worked example: drag A to (2, 3) and B to (8, 11) — the panel above should read Midpoint = (5, 7) and Distance ≈ 10.0. Try to predict the numbers before you drag, then check yourself.

Segment Addition Postulate

If point B lies between A and C on the same line, then the two smaller pieces always add up to the whole:

AB + BC = AC

This is the rule that lets you solve for a missing length algebraically instead of measuring — e.g. if AB = 3x, BC = 2x + 4, and AC = 24, then 3x + 2x + 4 = 24, so x = 4.

Congruent Segments & Bisectors

Two segments are congruent (≅) when they have the same length — not necessarily the same position or direction. On a diagram, congruent segments are shown with matching tick marks (you'll see this again in the Congruent Triangles lab).

A segment bisector is any point, ray, line, or plane that cuts a segment into two congruent halves — exactly what the midpoint M does above.

Angle Pairs

MA.912.GR.1 — Complementary, supplementary, and vertical angles.

Drag the handle to rotate the angle and watch its partner update live.

Complementary

Two angles whose measures add to 90°. They don't need to touch — any two angles totaling 90° are complementary.

Supplementary & Linear Pairs

Two angles whose measures add to 180° are supplementary. When they're also adjacent (share a vertex and a side) and form a straight line — like the two angles on the horizontal line in the diagram — they're specifically called a linear pair.

Vertical Angles

When two lines cross, they form two pairs of angles directly across from each other at the vertex. Those pairs are always congruent — this is the Vertical Angles Theorem, and it's true no matter what θ is.

Angle Addition Postulate

If a ray splits a bigger angle into two smaller ones, the pieces add up to the whole — just like the Segment Addition Postulate, but for angles:

m∠ABD + m∠DBC = m∠ABC

In the diagram, all four angles around the vertex always add up to 360° — that's what keeps θ, its supplement, and their vertical partners locked together as you drag.

Congruent Triangles

MA.912.GR.3 — Same size, same shape. Proving it without measuring everything.

Two triangles are congruent (≅) when all corresponding sides and angles match exactly. You almost never need to check all six — pick a postulate below to see the minimum proof each one needs.

Side-Side-Side (SSS)

If all three sides of one triangle equal all three sides of another, the triangles must be congruent — the shape is locked in.

Matching marks (tick marks) show which parts are given congruent.

Corresponding Parts

A↔A' · B↔B' · C↔C' · AB↔A'B' · BC↔B'C' · CA↔C'A'

CPCTC — Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent. Once you've proven two triangles congruent using any postulate above, CPCTC lets you conclude that every remaining pair of sides and angles matches too — even ones you never measured.

Naming order matters: writing △ABC ≅ △A'B'C' claims A↔A', B↔B', C↔C' specifically — always read off corresponding parts in that same letter order.

Similar Triangles

MA.912.GR.4 — Same shape, proportional size.

Similar triangles (~) have the exact same angle measures but sides scaled by a constant scale factor (k). Drag the slider — watch the small triangle stay perfectly proportional to the big one, with matching angle arcs proving the shape never changes.

0.60

Matching arc colors mark equal angles — cyan pairs with cyan, magenta with magenta, no matter the size.

How to prove similarity

  • AA~ — two pairs of angles are congruent (the third pair matches automatically, since angles sum to 180°).
  • SAS~ — two pairs of sides are proportional and the included angle is congruent.
  • SSS~ — all three pairs of sides are proportional.

Right-Triangle Trigonometry

MA.912.T.2 — SOH · CAH · TOA, live, in 3D.

Trigonometry is just a set of fixed ratios between the sides of a right triangle, one ratio per angle measure. Change θ and every side length can change — but for that same θ, the ratio of any two sides never does. That's the whole idea.

35°

Drag to orbit the 3D triangle + unit circle. Cyan = opposite, magenta = adjacent, yellow = hypotenuse (radius).

Which side is which?

  • Hypotenuse — always the side opposite the right angle. Always the longest side. Never changes, no matter which acute angle you're looking from.
  • Opposite — the side directly across from angle θ; it doesn't touch θ at all.
  • Adjacent — the other leg; it touches θ but isn't the hypotenuse.
Swap which acute angle you call θ, and "opposite" and "adjacent" swap too — only the hypotenuse stays fixed.

A flat, exact-scale view of the same triangle — easier to read the numbers precisely.

Going Backwards: Inverse Trig

SOH-CAH-TOA finds a side when you know θ. To find the angle when you already know two sides, undo the function with its inverse (your calculator's sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹ buttons):

θ = sin⁻¹(opposite / hypotenuse)
θ = cos⁻¹(adjacent / hypotenuse)
θ = tan⁻¹(opposite / adjacent)
Real-world framing: the angle of elevation is the angle up from the horizontal to something above you (e.g. the top of a tree); the angle of depression is the angle down from horizontal to something below you (e.g. a boat from a cliff). Both are just θ in a right triangle — set up opposite/adjacent/hypotenuse the same way.
